Mechanistic interpretability · a replication of Nanda et al. (2023)

Watching a transformer grok

A tiny transformer learns to add numbers on a 113-hour clock. First it simply memorises its training examples. Then, thousands of steps later and with no change to the training set, it suddenly generalises. Scrub through training to watch the algorithm it discovers take shape in its weights: numbers arranged in circles, built from a handful of waves.

Code on GitHub How it works

1 · The task

Addition on a clock

Each example is two numbers a, b from 0 to 112, fed in as three tokens [a, b, =]. The model must answer (a + b) mod 113: addition on a clock with 113 hours. It trains on a random 30% of the 12,769 possible pairs and is tested on the other 70%.

It's a one-layer transformer (4 attention heads, a 512-neuron MLP, about 227k parameters) trained with a strong weight decay. At step 0 its weights are random, so every panel shows noise.

2 · Memorisation · steps 0 –

A perfect score, for the wrong reasons

Within about 150 steps, training accuracy hits 100%. Test loss doesn't fall: it climbs to about , far worse than random guessing (ln 113 ≈ 4.7). The network has built a lookup table and is confidently wrong about anything it hasn't seen.

3 · Circuit formation · to step

Nothing seems to happen, but something does

For thousands of steps the headline curves barely move. Underneath, weight decay keeps squeezing, and the norm falls steadily. In the spectrum, four frequencies, , slowly rise above the noise, and the k = 1 ring begins to organise itself.

The progress measures (now switched on) make this visible. Restricted loss keeps only the Fourier part of the model's output, and it falls steadily. Excluded loss removes that part, and it rises. A new algorithm is being built underneath the memorised one.

4 · Grokking · step

The sudden part

At around step , the Fourier circuit becomes strong enough to produce the answer on its own. Test accuracy goes from near-chance to over 95% in a few hundred steps, and test loss eventually falls by over seven orders of magnitude.

It looks like a flash of insight. It isn't. As the previous step showed, the circuit has been forming for thousands of steps. This is just the moment it starts to dominate.

5 · Cleanup · to step

Weight decay clears out the lookup table

Once the general algorithm is doing the work, the memorisation weights only add cost. Weight decay removes them: the norm drops to about , the noise floor of the spectrum collapses, and every ring snaps into focus.

6 · The algorithm

Adding numbers by rotating

Each key frequency k places number a on a circle at angle w·a, where w = 2πk/113. With consecutive numbers connected, each +1 jumps k positions around the ring.

Adding a and b is then just combining two rotations: the attention and MLP layers compute cos(w(a+b)) and sin(w(a+b)) using trig identities. The output layer then scores each candidate answer c with cos(w(a+b−c)), which peaks exactly when c ≡ a+b. Each frequency on its own gives a rough answer; the four peaks only line up at the correct one.

7 · Why weight decay matters

Every parameter pays rent

Memorising and the Fourier algorithm both fit the training data perfectly, so the training loss alone gives the model no reason to switch. Weight decay does: it charges for the size of every weight. A few waves shared across all inputs are far cheaper than nearly 4,000 stored answers, so the optimiser drifts toward the general solution. Without weight decay, grokking is dramatically slower or doesn't happen at all.

Reading the panels

Loss
Cross-entropy on the training set (30% of pairs) and the held-out test set (70%), on a log step axis so the early memorisation and late grokking both fit. Shaded bands mark the three phases. The progress measures follow Nanda et al.: they split the model's output into its key-frequency Fourier part and everything else.
Weight norm
√(sum of every parameter squared). AdamW with weight decay 1.0 shrinks every weight a little each step, so the network has to keep "paying" for everything it stores.
Fourier spectrum
The embedding gives each number a a 128-dimensional vector. Read each dimension as a function of a, rewrite it as a sum of waves cos(2πka/113) and sin(2πka/113), and measure how much of each frequency k is used. A random embedding uses every frequency a little; the trained one uses just .
Ring
Every number's embedding, projected onto the plane spanned by the final model's cos and sin directions for frequency k. The axes are fixed for every frame, so you're seeing structure emerge, not the camera moving. The dashed circle is radius 1, where a fully formed ring sits.
PCA
The two directions along which the final embedding varies most. They turn out to be the k = 1 ring. "Per frame" recomputes PCA for each checkpoint, aligned to its neighbour so it doesn't flip; early on, its top directions are noise.

The setup

  • p = 113; inputs [a, b, =]; the answer is read from the final position.
  • One layer: dmodel = 128, 4 heads of 32, MLP 512 (ReLU), learned positional embeddings, no LayerNorm, untied embed/unembed.
  • Full-batch AdamW, lr 10−3, weight decay 1.0, β = (0.9, 0.98), 10-step warmup; seed 0.
  • Trained for steps (about 4½ minutes on a consumer GPU), with 508 checkpoints saved; the 300 frames shown are packed most densely where the curves move fastest.
